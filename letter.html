A Letter from 2055 to 2025

Written thirty years in your future from the foothills of Mount Makiling. A message about what survived, what changed, and how learning to live in balance kept our community alive.

Dispatched from Los Baños, Laguna

Canopy Terrace, Sector 4 • Foothills of Mount Makiling

SEPTEMBER 11, 2055
Dear Citizens of 2025,

I am sitting on the open breezeway of our 40-story residential tower in Los Baños. The morning air is crisp and cool. Below me, there are no highway traffic jams, no diesel jeepneys coughing black exhaust, and no endless rows of concrete subdivisions eating into the mountainside. Instead, the forest of Mount Makiling flows directly through our buildings. Tall native Narra trees and green hanging vines climb our balconies, and in the distance, Laguna de Bay shines with clean water we can actually swim in and fish from.

If you could see our town today, you might think it looks like a futuristic paradise. But I want to tell you the honest truth about how we got here, because the path was not easy.

Back in the 2020s and 2030s, we made the mistake of thinking technology alone could solve climate change. We built bigger air conditioners, bought electric cars, and paved more roads. But the heatwaves got hotter, reaching unbearable highs, and the super-typhoons flooded our roads every single year. Laguna de Bay was dying from chemical runoffs, and our mountain slopes were being stripped bare.

The turning point came when our university, UPLB, and our community made a radical decision: we had to stop acting like humans are separate from nature. We had to embrace Human Ecology—the simple truth that our towns, our bodies, our economy, and our forests are part of one single living system.

So we changed how we lived:

  • We stopped spreading outward and built upward: We replaced wide suburban sprawl with vertical green towers made of self-healing bio-concrete. This allowed us to give 78% of the land back to the wild forest and wildlife corridors.
  • We cleaned our lake: Instead of dumping sewage into Laguna de Bay, we planted wide shoreline wetlands that naturally filter all water using native reeds and clean bio-filters. Today, our fisherfolk harvest fresh fish every morning.
  • We eliminated dirty fuel: We banned combustion engines. All our transportation runs on quiet, elevated electric skyway pods powered 100% by clean geothermal energy from the mountain and solar power.

Our public health has never been better. People in Los Baños live an average of 82 healthy years because we breathe clean mountain air and eat fresh vegetables grown on tower balconies.

Yet, our life is not free of struggle. To prevent overusing our natural resources, we live under strict ecological rationing. Every resident has a daily limit on water and power. Every morning, we check our family monitor: 65 liters of fresh water each day. We negotiate with our neighbors, we share what we have, and we hold weekly community council meetings to make sure no family is left behind.

To everyone reading this in 2025: do not give up on the future. The choices you make right now—how you protect your trees, how you clean your waterways, and how you look after your neighbors—will determine whether you inherit ruin or build a living city like ours.

With hope and solidarity across time,

Andrei Josh B. De Castro
